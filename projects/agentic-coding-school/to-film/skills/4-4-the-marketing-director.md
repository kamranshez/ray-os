## The Marketing Director

Content director handles what you create. Operations manager handles your money. Now we're building the employee that gets you in front of people — the marketing director.

And this one matters because for most solopreneurs and small businesses, marketing is the thing that falls off first. You know you should be posting on LinkedIn. You know you should be following up on emails. You know you should have a content calendar. But you're busy doing the actual work, and marketing slips.

So we build skills that handle it.

### The LinkedIn Skill (0:00–3:30)

I want a skill that runs my entire LinkedIn strategy from one command. Not "write me a LinkedIn post" — that's one post. I want a system. Ideation, variations, and a calendar.

> [SCREEN: Claude terminal]

> [TYPE: /skill-creator]

> [TYPE: "Build a LinkedIn content skill. Given my niche, it should generate 10-12 post ideas based on what's trending and what positions me as an authority. For the top ideas, write two variations of each post in my voice. Then organize everything into a 2-week posting calendar — one post per weekday. Pull voice from brand-context/voice-tone.md and messaging from brand-context/messaging-pillars.md."]

Skill Creator asks the usual questions. "What's your niche? What kind of posts perform best for you — stories, frameworks, hot takes? Do you want to include engagement hooks? How long should posts be?"

I tell it: AI and automation for business owners. Mix of frameworks and real stories. Keep posts under 200 words — LinkedIn rewards short and punchy. And yes, include a question at the end to drive comments.

> [SHOW: the skill being built — process steps, references to brand-context/]

Now I run it.

> [TYPE: "Plan my LinkedIn content for the next two weeks"]

> [SHOW: Claude running the skill — researching trends, generating ideas, writing variations]

It pulls trending topics in my niche. Cross-references them with my messaging pillars — so every idea ties back to what I actually want to be known for. Generates 12 ideas, each with a one-line summary and a relevance score.

Then it writes two variations of the top-scoring posts. Different hooks, different angles, same core message. This is useful because you can A/B test which version performs better, or just pick the one that feels right.

> [SHOW: the output — 12 ideas with scores, 8 written posts (2 variations each for top 4), and a calendar]

And at the bottom — a posting calendar. Two weeks laid out. Which post goes on which day. The highest-scoring ones go earlier in the week when LinkedIn engagement is highest.

Twelve ideas. Eight written posts. A two-week calendar. From one command. And every post sounds like me because it's pulling from my voice profile.

### The Email Drafter (3:30–5:30)

The second marketing skill is simpler but equally useful. An email drafter that connects to Gmail.

> [SCREEN: Claude terminal]

> [TYPE: /skill-creator]

> [TYPE: "Build an email drafter skill. It should connect to my Gmail, pull recent conversations that need responses, and draft replies. For cold outreach, I give it the recipient and context and it writes the email in my voice. For follow-ups, it checks thread history and drafts an appropriate next message."]

The key here is the Gmail connector we set up in the morning briefing lesson. The email skill uses that same connection to read thread context before drafting. It doesn't write blind — it knows what's already been said.

> [TYPE: "Draft a follow-up to my conversation with Sarah about the consulting project — she hasn't replied in 5 days"]

> [SHOW: Claude reading the email thread, then drafting a follow-up]

It reads the thread. Sees what we last discussed. Drafts a follow-up that references the specific conversation — not a generic "just checking in" but "following up on the pricing structure we discussed on the 15th — wanted to see if you had a chance to review the two options."

That's the difference between a skill with context and a prompt without it. The Gmail connection means the skill knows the conversation history. The brand context means it writes like you. Together, you get emails that sound like you actually wrote them.

### Why This Replaces Real Spend (5:30–7:00)

Now I want to put some numbers on this. Because the marketing director isn't just a convenience — it's a genuine cost replacement.

Before skills, running a small business marketing operation meant hiring people. A part-time copywriter for content and email — maybe $2,500 a month. A freelance creative director for strategy and planning — another $1,000. Miscellaneous freelancers for one-off tasks — another $1,000. That's $4,500 a month. $54,000 a year.

With a Claude subscription and the skills we've built in this class? $100 a month. $1,200 a year. That's a 97.8% reduction in operational cost for the marketing function.

Now — I want to be honest. These skills don't replace a senior marketing strategist who understands your market at a deep level. They don't replace relationship-based selling. They don't replace the creative spark that comes from a great human collaborator.

What they replace is the execution layer. The repetitive labor of generating post ideas, writing first drafts, organizing calendars, drafting routine emails. The stuff that takes time but doesn't require genius. That's what skills handle — and they handle it in seconds instead of hours.

### The Full Department (7:00–8:00)

So let's step back and look at what we've built in this chapter so far.

A morning briefing employee that starts your day and an update skill that ends it. A content director that plans and scripts your content. An operations manager that handles receipts and invoices. And now a marketing director that runs your LinkedIn strategy and drafts your emails.

Every one of these pulls from your brand context. Every one uses the architecture we learned in Chapter 2 — under 200 lines, reference files, good descriptions. And they're starting to work together — the research skill feeds into the content director, the brand context flows through everything.

This is what a one-person operation looks like when it's skill-powered. Not one person doing everything. One person directing AI employees that each handle their area.
