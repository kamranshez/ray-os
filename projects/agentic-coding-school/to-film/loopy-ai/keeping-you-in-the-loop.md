Stub for the human-facing half of governance. [[mission-command]] is how intent flows *down* into the loops. This segment is how work flows *back up* to you — at a rate one human can actually absorb.

## Thesis

Once you accept that **you are the bottleneck, not the agent count** ([[intro]], [[loop-stack]] L7), the delivery interface stops being an afterthought and becomes the design problem. A loop that does perfect work and dumps it where you'll never see it is a loop that doesn't exist. The orphaned tweet line from [[loop-design-as-craft]] is the whole segment:

> The next moat isn't a better model. It's the cleanest pipe into your decision surface.

The failure this prevents: ten terminals, thirty overnight diffs, and a human who can't keep up — so they either rubber-stamp (cognitive debt) or freeze. The fix is not fewer loops. It's a deliberately engineered pipe.

## The two things a loop can hand you

Name them on camera, because they want different channels and different cadences.

1. **Artifacts** — finished or near-finished work. Drafts, PRs, reports, slide decks. These want a **single channel** and a **batched cadence**. One inbox, not thirty terminals.
2. **Decisions** — the things the loop *can't* or *shouldn't* settle alone. Irreversible actions, ambiguous calls, anything high-stakes. These want **interrupt cadence** and a crisp "here's the call, here are the options, here's my recommendation."

Most people's loops surface neither cleanly — work is scattered, decisions are buried inside walls of log. Separating the two is the move.

## The menu of ways to stay in the loop

- **One channel, not thirty windows.** Pick a pipe and make every loop write to it: a Telegram/Slack channel, an email digest, a review queue, a kanban board. The point is you check *one* place, not hunt across sessions. (Parsons' morning brief + worker kanban; Boris's "surfaces three drafts a day.")
- **Batch on a cadence.** A 6am digest of everything that happened overnight beats real-time pings you'll learn to ignore. Cadence matched to the work: hourly, daily, Sunday. Pairs with the [[governance-primitives]] weekly fleet-health report — same instinct, different payload (that one is fleet health, this one is *your work output*).
- **Surface decisions, not transcripts.** The loop hands up the *call*, not the diff that led to it. "I can ship A or B, A is faster but loses property X — recommend A. Your call." Cheap to read, fast to answer.
- **Reversibility gates what auto-ships vs. waits for you.** Parsons' rule: *is this reversible without embarrassment?* If yes, the loop ships it and logs it. If no — sending an email, a prod migration, a public post — it stops and hands it to you. This is the dial between autonomy and oversight, and it's per-action, not per-loop.
- **Format for absorption, not for the machine.** Slides and step-by-step beats a wall of diff. *"The cleanest pipe into your decision surface"* is a literal instruction: design the report so a tired human at 6am can act on it in seconds.
- **Never let a loop close its own ticket.** The human owns the final close. Not because the loop is wrong, but because closing is how *you* stay current with your own system — the antidote to [[loop-design-as-craft]]'s cognitive-debt risk.

## The diagnostic

If you can't keep up with the output of **one** loop, adding a second loop (or six parallel agents) makes it worse, not better. The bottleneck is your decision surface, and more throughput upstream just floods it faster. Fix the pipe before you add agents. This is the practical, operational version of the [[intro]] thesis — and the reason this segment sits in Closing, not in The Climb.
