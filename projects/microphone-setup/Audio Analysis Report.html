The Problem

A viewer reported that the audio sounded boomy on headphones, making it difficult to understand. Spectral analysis of the original recording confirmed that 77.7% of all audio energy was concentrated in the bass band (80–250 Hz).

This is caused by proximity effect — a natural bass boost that occurs when speaking close to a directional microphone (Sennheiser MKH 50, supercardioid). Auphonic's loudness normalization then amplified the already bass-heavy signal by ~12 dB uniformly, making the problem worse.

Key Findings

1. MKH 50 Low-Cut Filter = Biggest Impact

Engaging the mic's hardware low-cut switch dropped bass from 75.7% → 64.8% in the raw recording. This was by far the most effective single change.

2. Auphonic's Leveler Pushes Bass Back Up (Slightly)

After Auphonic processing, bass went from 64.8% → 69.8%. The adaptive leveler and loudness normalization add about 5% bass energy back. Turning the leveler off only saved 0.7% — so the normalization is the main contributor, not the leveler.

3. 9 dB vs 6 dB Noise Reduction = Negligible Difference

Frequency balance was virtually identical (69.8% vs 69.3%). The only difference is a slightly higher noise floor with 6 dB (~3.5 dB more ambient noise). Use 6 dB for a more natural sound if your room is quiet.

4. Auphonic Filtering Helps But Can't Replace the Mic Filter

Even with Auphonic's adaptive high-pass enabled, the processing chain adds bass back. The hardware filter at the source is essential.

5. Premiere Pro (Loudness Only) Beats Auphonic

Simply raising loudness in Premiere Pro — with no other processing — preserved the raw mic balance perfectly at 64.8% bass. Auphonic's processing chain actually added 5% bass (69.8%). Skipping Auphonic entirely and using Premiere Pro produces a better-balanced result.

6. The MKH 50 Is a Boom Mic, Not a Podcast Mic

The MKH 50 is designed to be used on a boom at ~50cm distance, not close-miked like an SM7B. At 50cm overhead (current setup), the remaining bass is primarily from room acoustics — low-frequency standing waves, desk reflections, and wall reflections — which the MKH 50 faithfully captures due to its flat bass response down to 50 Hz. The SM7B doesn't pick this up because dynamic mics are less sensitive. This is the MKH 50 doing its job too well.

Key Takeaway

The SM7B final output is significantly better balanced for speech — only 48.4% bass vs 69.8% on the MKH 50. Interestingly, the SM7B raw recording was worse (81.1% bass), but Auphonic reshaped it far more aggressively (81% → 48%). With the MKH 50, Auphonic barely moved the needle (64.8% → 69.8%, actually adding bass).

This suggests Auphonic's processing interacts differently with each mic's frequency profile. The SM7B's broader midrange presence may give Auphonic more to work with when rebalancing.

Optional Further Improvements

  • Room treatment — moving blankets on walls behind your monitor, bass traps in corners. The remaining bass energy is mostly room acoustics, not proximity effect
  • Desk treatment — a thick desk mat or pad between you and the mic reduces desk reflections
