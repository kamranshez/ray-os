> A [[deck-note-type|deck]]: techniques from three sales operators, combined and sorted by the situation you would actually use them in.

## The three voices

Three people who built sales careers from different angles agree on more than they disagree. Where they overlap is the signal.

- **[HL] The founder-tactician.** A former engineer who moved into direct B2B tech sales and now coaches reps and founders. Strongest on the *mechanics*: cold call and email structure, discovery calls, the shape of a sales cycle.
- **[KS] The academic-practitioner.** Kent Summers, who exited three startups and teaches B2B sales at MIT and Harvard. Strongest on the *system and mindset*: customer profiles, pipeline math, the psychology of the buyer-seller relationship.
- **[AH] The volume operator.** Alex Hormozi, who personally closed 4,000+ sales and scaled multiple sales teams. Strongest on *throughput and the close*: maximizing reps, handling objections, and the meta-skills that keep a seller consistent.

Read across them and a single journey appears: get your head right, pick who to sell to, fill the pipeline, run the conversation, move the deal, close it, and stay consistent. The cards below follow that order.

## 1. Mindset and first principles

##### Revenue first, everything else second `[KS]`
If you cannot acquire customers cost-effectively, nothing else you do as a founder matters. Once cash is flowing, the hundred other problems suddenly feel manageable. Treat learning to sell as the survival skill, not a thing to delegate later.
**Use when:** you are a founder tempted to perfect the product, the deck, or the org chart before you have proven you can sell.

##### Sales is an outcome, attitude and behavior are the inputs `[KS]`
You do not control whether someone buys. You control your attitude and your behavior. Treat the sale as a lagging outcome measure and your competence at helping people as the leading indicator. Helping reliably produces transactions as a side effect.
**Use when:** you are anxious about a specific deal closing. Redirect that energy onto inputs you actually control.

##### Some will, some won't, so what, next `[KS]`
B2B sales is a failure-driven profession: the real failure rate is closer to 97%. The job is not failure *avoidance*, it is failure *recovery*. You can lose 90% of your games and still keep your job, unlike almost any other role.
**Use when:** a no stings, or a "perfect fit" prospect ghosts you. Recite it and move to the next name.

##### Disqualify, don't just qualify `[KS]`
The old rule "always be qualifying" is half wrong. Genuinely good-fit prospects are easy to spot. The skill is spotting and removing the *bad* fits fast, by asking questions that let people opt in or opt out, not by making statements that push them along.
**Use when:** your pipeline is bloated with maybes and you are not sure who is real.

##### Whoever cares most about the prospect wins `[AH]`
The best closers genuinely care about the buyer more than the buyer cares about themselves in that moment. Because you have more context than they do, you can see past their limiting beliefs to the outcome they actually want. Buyers hear that intention in your tone, and it is the opposite of pushy.
**Use when:** you feel like you are "selling at" someone. Re-anchor on what happens to them if they do nothing.

##### People buy from people (P2P, not B2B) `[KS]`
Forget B2B versus B2C. All sales are person to person. People buy from people, not from companies. Every tactic downstream is really about a human relationship under a business pretext.
**Use when:** you are over-engineering process and forgetting there is a person on the other end.

## 2. Targeting: who to sell to

##### Marketing fills the water, sales catches the fish `[HL]`
Sales and marketing are different jobs. Marketing is getting a thousand fish to swarm your bait. Sales is the one-on-one fight to land each fish. Founders who confuse the two blast 10,000 low-quality emails, call it "activity" for investors, and catch almost nothing. Dial in marketing first and every individual sale gets easier.
**Use when:** outreach feels like grinding with no traction. Check whether the real problem is upstream targeting, not your pitch.

##### The three-ring customer profile `[KS]`
Define who you sell to in three layers. **Company profile**: industry, type, size, geography (acid test: it should be specific enough to generate a list of real street addresses). **Buyer profile**: the one job title that gains or loses the most, expressed as a use case. **Decision-making unit**: who influences, who decides, who holds the checkbook. You do not get this from a business plan, you chisel it down after talking to 100+ prospects.
**Use when:** you are starting outreach, or your conversion rates are mushy and you suspect you are talking to the wrong people.

##### Focus on one use case `[KS]`
Founders love to list three or four use cases. Chasing them in parallel is hugely dilutive. Pursue one until it is proven. The only real validation of a profile is purchase orders from *different* companies solving a *similar* problem (which is also the honest investor definition of MVP).
**Use when:** you are spread across segments and none is converting cleanly.

##### Position to your actual size `[HL]`
Your stage in the market should set your message. A 250-person company that tells a prospect it is "world-class, best of breed, the market leader" against Oracle gets laughed out of the late stage. Instead, name the two or three narrow things you genuinely do better because you are focused, and let the incumbent be huge. If you *are* the big player, do the reverse: deploy your army of case studies and specialists.
**Use when:** writing positioning or walking into a competitive deal against a much larger or much smaller rival.

##### Hire a salesperson only when the path is repeatable `[KS]`
Bringing in a professional salesperson too early is usually an accelerant to failure, not a fix. You are ready to hire when you can say "go get me 100 more customers just like these 12," not "go figure out who our customer is." The founder is always the first salesperson because only the founder collects the raw product-market feedback.
**Use when:** you are tempted to solve a sales gap with a hire instead of with your own reps.

## 3. Filling the pipeline: prospecting and outreach

##### The pipeline is a funnel with conversion gates `[KS]`
Leads enter the top and become **suspects** (filtered to your profile = MQLs), then **prospects** (contacted, buyer profile and interest confirmed = SQLs), then **opportunities** (actively engaged), then **customers**. Conversion rates should *increase* left to right. If they do not, your entry criteria are too loose. Two forces shape the funnel: triage (you remove people) and attrition (they remove you).
**Use when:** you want one number to debug. Falling conversion left to right tells you exactly where the criteria leak.

##### Maximize available time slots `[AH]`
Across thousands of businesses the single biggest lever on total sales was simply availability: the number of bookable time slots. Be available when the prospect is available, including odd hours and weekends. The business owner who says "no same-day, come back Tuesday" loses to the one who says "come in now."
**Use when:** you are capping your own calendar out of ego or convenience and wondering why volume is low.

##### Pull up appointments `[AH]`
Same-day appointments show up at higher rates and close higher because interest is hot. If a call is booked for Thursday and you have an open slot today, drag it forward. If a prospect has gone quiet and their slot is near, free it and refill it with someone responsive. This multiplies calls per day.
**Use when:** your calendar has gaps and stale future bookings sitting in it.

##### Two SOPs: on-call and off-call `[AH]`
Most teams have a script for what to say *on* a call and nothing for what to do *between* calls. The off-call SOP (follow up, drag appointments forward, cancel dead ones, work the kill list) is where your future opportunities come from. Write both and switch hats the moment a call ends.
**Use when:** reps are "working leads in the meantime" with no structure and output is inconsistent.

##### Don't sell over email, get them on a call `[KS] [HL]`
When a stranger emails you a problem you solve, do not solve it in the reply: you lose the person-to-person interaction and the reason for them to contact you again. The standard response is "this sounds like something we can help with, send me a few days and times and I'll confirm a call." For your own cold outreach, the goal of a call or email is identical: *start a conversation*, not pitch. State who you are, why you are reaching out, ask about what they do today, ask for 15-20 minutes.
**Use when:** you are tempted to write the five-paragraph value-prop email, or to solve a problem in writing.

##### Keep interactions brief and narrow `[KS]`
Diminishing returns on attention hit fast in sales. Keep each touch short, and keep it to one well-defined step. Resist stuffing multiple stages of your process into a single hour-long call: leave room for the relationship to build across touches.
**Use when:** you have an interested prospect on the line and feel the urge to cover everything at once.

##### Say "we," not "I" `[KS]`
A small but powerful word. "We" makes a prospect feel they are dealing with a company, not a lone individual. Appear bigger and more professional than you are.
**Use when:** you are a solo founder or tiny team and want to project credibility.

##### Add value in every interaction `[KS]`
You are ambushing people's calendars, so earn it. Bring a question, a piece of guidance, or an insight to every touch. Then people talk to you because you are useful, not out of courtesy.
**Use when:** scheduling any prospect touch. Ask what they get out of this specific 20 minutes.

##### Don't reach out on a bad day `[KS]`
The further a conversation goes, the more it costs to lose. On low days, do not interact with prospects: do admin, go to the movies. Save the important calls for your high-mojo days.
**Use when:** you are forcing yourself to dial while in a bad headspace.

##### Create your own pipeline with referrals `[AH]`
The best sellers do not just work the leads handed to them. At the right moment they ask "who do you know who is as awesome as you and would also benefit from this?" Phrasing it as "who do you know" forces a name, not a yes/no, and the compliment softens the ask. Referral close rates run 80-90%, so even a one-in-three referral rate can cut your cost to acquire a customer in half.
**Use when:** you have a happy or sold customer in front of you, or you simply need more top-of-funnel.

## 4. The conversation: discovery and calls

##### Spend the first 18-20 minutes asking, not pitching `[HL]`
The number one beginner mistake is talking about yourself. The second is asking one question, hearing a hint of a need, and immediately pitching. A great 30-minute discovery call is mostly you understanding what they do today, how big the problem is, why they have not solved it, and what else competes for their attention. You earn the right to pitch only after you understand their world.
**Use when:** any first real conversation with a qualified prospect.

##### Do five minutes of prep `[AH]`
Five minutes of research before a call makes you sound like a genius. Rule of thumb: prep for about 10% of a routine call's length. Open with what you found ("I saw your square footage layout, I think moving this could double your usable space") so you arrive with the problem *and* a contextual solution. Prep is how the small player out-credentials the big reputation.
**Use when:** before every call, especially when you are new and have no reputation to lean on.

##### Take notes and reference them next time `[AH]`
Public prep gets you the first call. Notes from that call prep you for the second. Opening call two with "you mentioned your daughters Sarah and Jessica are finishing in Colorado, and X is happening in the business, right?" proves your recommendations are contextual to *them*, which is the buyer's deepest worry. This is what turns a salesperson into a consultant.
**Use when:** running any multi-call cycle.

##### Listen twice as much as you talk `[AH]`
Across analyzed call recordings, the best sellers listen about twice as much as they talk. The talkative stereotype is what *bad* sellers look like. The best ones do not feel like salespeople: the buyer feels like they bought, not like they were sold.
**Use when:** you catch yourself monologuing or waiting for your turn to talk.

##### Answer questions with questions: be like smoke `[AH]`
Whoever is answering is the one being interrogated, and every answer gives the other side something to attack. So when asked "what makes you better than your competitors?" reply "what are you looking for?" then "why is that important to you?" People believe what *they* say, not what you say. Get them to articulate that your solution is a good idea.
**Use when:** a prospect probes you early, before trust exists. Hand the question back.

##### Build the relationship in order: empathy, trust, value, competence `[KS]`
There are four pillars and they only work in sequence. **Empathy** (convince them you understand their pain) leads to **trust** (you have their best interest in mind, including pointing them elsewhere if you are not a fit), which earns the right to discuss **value** in *their* terms, and only then **competence** (your product, your team). Jumping straight to competence is a fatal error: solving someone's nine-month problem in two minutes feels dismissive, not impressive.
**Use when:** you feel the pull to prove how good your product is. Check which pillar you are actually standing on.

##### Don't evangelize, that's marketing's job `[KS]`
If you find yourself teaching a prospect the basics of what you do, they are not qualified to take your time. Four disqualifiers: you are educating them from zero, the business case is not obvious to them, the problem's scale does not support your price, or it is simply not a priority. Spend your time converting knowledgeable people, not converting skeptics.
**Use when:** a conversation feels like a free training session. Move up to a decision maker or move on.

## 5. Moving the deal: cycle and timing

##### The technical sales cycle `[HL]`
A clean B2B cycle has shape: discovery call (understand, do not pitch), then a deeper technical call with more of their team plus a tailored demo, then a management call to confirm the problem and align, then a **time-bound free trial**, then procurement and legal. Each step expands the people involved.
**Use when:** you have a real opportunity and need to know what the next milestone should be.

##### Make trials time-bound with success criteria `[HL]`
The biggest mid-stage mistake is "here's a trial, let me know what you think." Momentum dies, they get stuck, they drift. Bound every trial to 2-4 weeks and agree up front on the success criteria that, if met, mean they buy. Get management and engineering aligned before it starts.
**Use when:** a prospect is excited and you are about to hand over access. Set the clock and the bar first.

##### Be willing to walk away `[HL] [KS]`
If a prospect will not bring other people to the call, will not agree on success criteria, or will not engage with the trial, that is a signal they are not serious. Move people *out* of the pipeline as confidently as you move them forward. Reconnect in a quarter when their situation may have changed.
**Use when:** you are pouring energy into someone who keeps not reciprocating.

##### Treat opportunities like perishable food `[KS]`
Manage timing by frequency. **Kitchen table**: deciding in days or weeks, gets 2-3 touches a week. **Refrigerator**: deciding in a couple of months, 1-2 touches a month. **Freezer**: next year's budget, every 3-6 months. Every morning you "open the fridge" and decide what gets eaten this week and what goes back in the freezer.
**Use when:** you have too many live deals and no system for who gets attention today.

##### Balance your activity, don't only chase top deals `[KS]`
Spending 100% of your time on the two or three deals most likely to close produces a Groundhog Day of feast-and-famine: by the time you win one, the rest of the funnel went cold. Spend half your time on top deals and half developing the rest. You give up the first six months of peaks for sustainable revenue, and deals start closing from places you never expected.
**Use when:** your revenue swings wildly between big wins and dry spells.

##### Book a meeting from a meeting (BAMFAM) `[AH]`
The best sellers never end a call without knowing when the next contact is. Never let a prospect fall into no-man's-land with "let's circle back offline." If timing is an obstacle, resolve it right there while both calendars are open, or surface the real concern ("how much is this costing you every day you don't fix it?").
**Use when:** ending any call with a live prospect.

##### Keep a visible kill list `[AH]`
Your highest-value prospects (the whales) live in the CRM like everyone else, but also somewhere you see them every day. They get extra attention and never slip through the cracks.
**Use when:** you have a handful of deals that would move the year and you cannot afford to forget them.

## 6. Closing and objections

##### Kill zombies up front: obstacles vs objections `[AH]`
An **obstacle** is raised before you mention price. An **objection** is the same concern after price, when the stakes are high and the clock is ticking. It is far easier to defuse a concern before money is on the table. Surface the spouse, the budget, the timing, the fit *early*, so by the time you ask for the sale everything is already handled.
**Use when:** you sense a landmine forming. Confront it now: if you cannot even raise it before price, it will detonate after.

##### The three root objections `[AH]`
Every objection collapses into three buckets. **Circumstances** (time, money, fit), which are the easy, reflexive blame. **Other people** (spouse, partner, boss). **Self** (doubt they can succeed). Good news: people usually start with circumstances, and as they move to "other people" then "myself," you are getting deeper down the onion toward the real decision maker.
**Use when:** you hear "I don't have the time/money." Treat it as the surface layer and keep going.

##### Break beliefs with stories and play it out `[AH]`
Memorize key stories and metaphors that dismantle each objection ("permission versus support" for the spouse block, "resourcefulness versus resources" for money). Then play the decision out, best case and worst case, into the future. "Worst case you can't afford food and you lose some weight, win-win. Best case you change your life forever. Is that a bet worth taking?" Playing it out takes the emotion out of the decision.
**Use when:** a prospect is frozen by fear of a wrong decision rather than by the facts.

##### Use tone and humor to defuse `[AH]`
The same words land completely differently depending on tone. A light, playful, even teasing delivery ("five minutes ago you said you had four hours a week, what happened to my four hours?") lets you ask hard questions without the prospect getting defensive. Then switch tone to get the real answer.
**Use when:** you need to confront something uncomfortable and don't want it to turn adversarial.

##### Ask for the sale again (looping) `[AH]`
If you do not ask, you do not get it. And asking *again* raises the odds, with one rule: only re-ask after you have *resolved* the concern. "Can't do Tuesdays" then "if we did Thursday, would that work?" then ask again. Resolve, ask, resolve, ask, as many times as you both have time, as long as you are addressing a real concern each loop.
**Use when:** you got a soft no but the underlying need is still real.

##### Never ask for the sale unless you know they'll say yes `[AH]`
Like a lawyer who never asks a question on the stand they don't know the answer to, a great closer reads the room and asks more questions when they sense hesitation rather than going for a premature close. "I'm feeling some hesitation around price, talk to me about that."
**Use when:** you are unsure if they are ready. Ask another question instead of asking for the card.

##### Acknowledge the objection, then ask the need behind the need `[HL]`
A simple, beginner-proof frame for any objection: acknowledge it as potentially valid, then ask what is underneath. "Totally understand, people do say we're expensive, and maybe we are. Compared to what you're doing today, why would you say that?" Often the real issue is that the need is not big enough or not fully understood.
**Use when:** you get hit with "too expensive" or "not the right time" and want a calm, honest opener.

##### Draw a line with pocket-pickers `[KS]`
Some "prospects" are just mining you for competitive intelligence. They ask great, knowledgeable questions but will never buy. Ask about *their* project, timeline, and what they are actually doing. Then draw a line: "We'd love to help, but in the context of your specific data, so let's do a low-cost pilot." If they won't spend even $5-20k, they were picking your pocket.
**Use when:** someone is unusually informed and curious but evasive about their own project.

## 7. Consistency and self-management

##### Consistency is a skill, not a mood `[AH]`
Most sellers are on, then off, motivated then demotivated. The gap between champions and everyone else is the ability to sustain performance over a long period. If you cannot stay consistent, that is a skill deficiency you can train, not a sign that "sales isn't for you." Enthusiasm is the same: maintaining it is a learnable skill.
**Use when:** you are questioning whether you are cut out for this during a slump.

##### Be a tiger, not a dog or a horse `[AH]`
Three types of seller. Dogs need constant feeding and attention or they starve. Horses gallop only while you whip them. Tigers will eat a full meal and still kill a passing bunny for sport, because they love the hunt. Aim to be the seller who would do it for free and happens to get paid.
**Use when:** you are evaluating your own drive, or hiring and deciding who to bet on.

##### Take every lead as free practice `[AH]`
Treating a "bad" lead as not worth your effort is like only doing warm-up reps: you are not actually practicing, and you build bad habits. The business paid for that lead either way. Take it seriously, sharpen the sword, and you either practice or you close. Both are wins. (The seller who volunteered for everyone's worst leads became the best, because he took the most reps.)
**Use when:** you are tempted to coast through a low-quality lead.

##### Track more than close rate `[AH]`
Close rate is just an outcome with no leading indicators. The best sellers track the whole funnel: percentage of leads booked, show rate, offer rate, close rate, average calls to close, average cash collected. Lifting your booking or show rate 20% is worth as much as lifting close rate 20%. Skill level shows in the quantity and quality of metrics someone tracks.
**Use when:** you want to improve but only know your close number. Instrument the steps before it.

##### Take 100% of the control, never blame circumstances `[AH]`
The best sellers (and marketers) never blame the leads, the commission structure, or the market. They focus only on what they control. When everyone on a team takes full ownership of their outcomes, the team wins. Genuine marketing feedback goes one-on-one to a manager with data, never as a complaint on a group call.
**Use when:** you hear yourself (or a rep) starting a sentence with "the leads are bad."

##### Double down on the one channel that's working `[HL]`
A beginner who gets traction on cold calls immediately wants to also learn email. Resist it. Until you have completely saturated the one thing that is working, double and triple down on it. Only once it is systematized and you have leads to spare should you spend even 10% of your time experimenting with a second channel.
**Use when:** something is finally working and you feel the itch to add a new tactic.

##### Drill one thing at a time `[AH]`
No one can fix nine things at once: it is discouraging and nothing improves. Like any skills coach, work one part of the script per session (just the intro today, just the close tomorrow), drill it three times right in a row, then move on. And "breathe the script": memorize it so completely that you can be fully present and listening instead of rehearsing your next line in your head.
**Use when:** coaching yourself or a rep, or when your delivery feels stiff because you are thinking about what to say.

## Where the three voices converge

When operators this different agree, treat it as the core of the craft:

- **Start a conversation, don't pitch.** `[HL]` and `[KS]` both say the goal of any first touch is dialogue, and `[AH]`'s "be like smoke" is the same instinct: get them talking, not you presenting.
- **Understand before you prescribe.** "Ask for the first 20 minutes" `[HL]`, "empathy before competence" `[KS]`, and "listen twice as much as you talk" `[AH]` are one idea in three accents.
- **Disqualify fast and walk away.** `[KS]`'s "disqualify" and "perishable food," `[HL]`'s "be willing to walk away," and `[AH]`'s "track data to score leads" all push you to spend time only where it converts.
- **Control your inputs, not the outcome.** `[KS]`'s attitude-and-behavior and `[AH]`'s "take 100% of the control" are the same discipline.
